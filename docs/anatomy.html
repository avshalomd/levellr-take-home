How Community Insights answers, and why it is built this way

A chatbot for a game studio's Community & Marketing Manager, over two weeks of the Veil of Ages Discord. Ask what players are excited about, frustrated by, or saying about an update, and get an answer grounded in the messages: each claim linked to the message behind it and checked, or a plain "the data cannot answer that". This page takes it apart: the data, each decision and what else was on the table, and what the evals measured.

Production is frozen at the tag, and commits after it change docs only. Code, public: github.com/avshalomd/levellr-take-home. Every question spends the brief's capped Gemini key.

The choices that shape the answers

Key design decisions

Eight decisions decide how an answer is found, counted and checked. Each card gives the choice, what else was on the table, and the number that makes the case. All 33 are listed under Decisions.

D3Grouping →

A conversation is one channel's 15-minute session, kept whole; a session over 40 messages is cut at its longest pause 20 to 40 messages in.

Instead of
Reply trees, or fixed windows of 30 messages.
Why
63% of messages are not replies, so reply trees leave most of the data out, and fixed windows cut exchanges mid-way. 159 sessions over 30 messages hold 67% of all messages, too much for one label. Result: 2,362 conversations, median 4 messages, every message in exactly one.
D6Labels →

Labels are one yes/no probability per topic from Jev, stored, and counted at 0.5 in SQL.

Instead of
One topic per conversation as a hard verdict, or free-text tags from an LLM.
Why
588 of the 2,362 conversations (25%) sit in two or more topics, and a probability keeps how sure the label is. A full labelling run cost $0.23 to $0.24, and re-asking one topic is priced at about $0.06, so the team can relabel without an engineer.
D16 · D17Labels →

Topics say what a conversation is about, flags what kind it is; a flag means a main thread of the conversation.

Instead of
One flat list of labels, and flags that ask "does anyone ...".
Why
"Frustrations about Domains" crosses a subject with a kind, which a flat list cannot group. Under "anyone", 20.1% of conversations carried both excited and frustrated; with "main thread", 0.7%.
D10Retrieval →

Two retrieval tools, routed by the question's shape: scan has Jev read a whole slice; find is hybrid keyword + vector search with a Jev rerank.

Instead of
One vector search for everything.
Why
"What are people frustrated about" has no keyword, and top-k samples a slice instead of reading it. On the retrieval eval, hybrid + Jev scored nDCG 0.78 against 0.29 for vectors alone, and routing named things to find took lookups to 6 of 6 in the 35-question run.
D11Retrieval, the other tools →

Every number comes from SQL or a tool result; the agent never writes SQL.

Instead of
Trust the model's arithmetic and its tags.
Why
Models miscount, and a community manager repeats the number in a meeting. The 7 aggregate eval questions are graded against SQL run at eval time, and a figure tagged to a tool that never ran fails its claim.
D12Claim check →

Every cited claim is checked: the handle exists, a tool showed it, and Jev says the message supports it; a weak claim is rewritten once and kept only if better.

Instead of
Trust the model's citations.
Why
A citation that does not support its sentence is worse than none. In the 35-question run, 129 of 131 cited claims (98.5%) were backed by the messages they cite.
D1Storage →

One Neon Postgres with pgvector holds messages, conversations, labels, vectors and the full-text index.

Instead of
A separate vector store beside the database.
Why
A search, a label filter and a count are one SQL query over the same rows, with nothing to keep in sync: find runs its keyword list, its vector list and their fusion in one statement.
D9Agent →

Gemini 2.5 Flash runs the agent, kept after measuring 3.8 Flash on the same questions.

Instead of
Gemini 3.8 Flash, likely better at using tools.
Why
On the same 35 questions 3.8 was judged right on 35 against 34, at about three times the median latency (19.4 s against 6.7 s) and with 68 tool calls against 42. 2.5 spends less of the capped key, and one env var switches it.
The brief, the data, the unit

A pulse for the community team, from one Discord's last two weeks

The brief: build a chatbot a Community & Marketing Manager can ask about the community's messages, to shape content and comms. It asks for a useful answer grounded in the messages and calls anything more a bonus. The data is 25,555 pseudonymised Discord messages from one gaming community, each with a channel, a pseudonymous author, a time, reactions and a reply parent.

The questions the brief names

What have players been frustrated about in the last few days?What are people most excited about right now?What should we post about this week?

The data, measured

25,555 messages795 authors, no bots13 Sep 19:44 to 27 Sep 19:30 UTC37% are repliesreactions on 4.5%, at most 8median text 40 charactersno permalinks

The game is a renamed Assassin's Creed. Bushido is the current game; its final content update (Title Update 1.1.11) landed on the night of 17 into 18 Sep with the Ebontide quest and the Domains rogue-lite mode. Tides Remastered is an upcoming remaster, Hollow an upcoming game.

"Now" is the last message, 27 Sep 19:30 UTC, not the server's clock (D2). "The last 3 days" runs from 24 Sep 19:30 to 27 Sep 19:30, to the minute; the first prompt counted calendar days, which gave four. Against the wall clock every relative question would come back empty or wrong the day after it was sent.

Dates written inside messages do not match the timestamps. A message stamped 27 Sep says "Today is 25th of June", and Tides Remastered's launch is "July 9th" in messages stamped 18 to 27 Sep. The timestamps look shifted forward by about three months. The app trusts the timestamps, so a date quoted from a message can contradict the window it sits in (P8, open in v1.0).

Messages by channel

Channels map to products, and the export names channels only, so the agent's instructions say once which channel is about which game.

The unit, and what a question can count

Stored and counted
  • A message is one Discord message (channel, pseudonymous author, UTC time, reactions, reply parent). It is what answers cite.
  • A conversation is a piece of one channel's activity: split at 15-minute pauses; a session over 40 messages is cut at its longest pause 20 to 40 messages in, repeatedly, and the last piece keeps the remainder. It is what the agent searches, reads, labels and counts, dated by its first message. Every message is in exactly one. 2,362 conversations: median 4 messages, p90 33, max 40.
  • Counts of conversations, their messages, people and reactions, by day, week, month, topic or channel.
  • Engagement: distinct authors + replies + reactions per conversation, because reactions alone are too sparse (D5).
Said, not counted
  • Anything outside these 11 channels: Reddit, Steam reviews, sales, revenue.
  • Anything before 13 Sep, and readers who never wrote.
  • Reach (reactions are on 4.5% of messages, at most 8), or intent beyond the words.
  • A count of conversations is not a count of people; answers say which unit a number is in.
Decision · the unit of retrieval (D3, D4)

A conversation is one channel's 15-minute session, kept whole, or cut where the talk paused if it runs long

A single chat line is a poor unit: "tru", "yipee!" and "Ahh, myth busted" mean nothing alone, and a reply's meaning lives in what it answers. So each channel is split into sessions at 15-minute silences, and almost every session is kept whole. Below is a real stretch of #new-release-discussion on 24 Sep, 08:10 to 13:15 UTC: 156 messages in three sessions, a short one of 4 messages, a 125-message Domains session and a 27-message one. Switch strategies to see how each one cuts it.

The unit, in plain terms

Most conversations

One 15-minute session, kept whole

Per channel, a new conversation starts after 15 minutes of silence. A session of 40 messages or fewer is one conversation as it is, however it is shaped: a question and its answer, a run of one-liners, two people talking.

1,809 of the 1,933 sessions are kept whole, 77% of the 2,362 conversations. They are small: median 2 messages, and 1,098 of them hold 1 to 3 messages. In the timeline below: conv1803, 4 messages (a lore question about Hana's farsight and the reply that answers it), and conv1829, 27 messages.

Long sessions

Cut at the longest pause

A session over 40 messages is cut into pieces. Each cut goes at the longest pause 20 to 40 messages into the piece, and the last piece keeps the remainder.

124 sessions run over 40 messages. They make 553 pieces, 51 of them under 20. In the timeline below: the 125-message Domains session, in five pieces.

What replies do. Replies never decide the grouping. They only bring in a reply's parent that sits in another conversation, shown as context beside the reply and never counted in its conversation (D4). Why reply trees are not the unit: 63% of messages are not replies, so they would belong to no conversation; merged into the sessions, replies link sessions into chains, and the largest group holds 1,989 messages.

What short conversations cost. A one-line session is its own conversation: 619 sessions are a single message. Nothing special is done for them: they are labelled, embedded and searched as they are, and a lone one-liner carries little meaning. Two things give them context. A reply's parent from another conversation rides along in the transcript Jev and the agent read (868 of the whole sessions carry one); conv1534, for example, is one message, "same", shown under the "have it on PC" it answers from 37 minutes earlier. And the excited, frustrated and help flags read a one- or two-message conversation as the message itself. The embedding uses the conversation's own messages only.

Result: 1,809 whole sessions + 553 pieces = 2,362 conversations, and every message is in exactly one.

What the agent and Jev read for one conversation

It opens on conv1803, a short session kept whole: a lore question, an aside, and the reply that answers it. Click a dot in the long session to open one of its pieces. The transcript carries each message's short handle, so a citation can still point at one message. A parent from an earlier piece is shown as context, never counted twice (D4).

The rule, exactly as ingest/group.py runs it

No model is involved: plain Python over timestamps and reply links, tested in ingest/test_group.py, so the same data always gives the same conversations.

  1. Sessions. Per channel, in time order, a new session starts wherever two messages are more than 15 minutes apart.
  2. Pieces. A session over 40 messages is cut. From the piece's start i, while more than 40 messages remain, the cut goes before the message j in [i+20, i+40] with the longest pause since the one before it (ties: the earliest). The last piece keeps the remainder.
  3. Context. A reply whose parent sits in an earlier piece or session gets the parent attached as context: shown in the transcript, never a member, so it is never counted or cited from two conversations.
  4. Counts per conversation. Messages, distinct authors, replies inside it, reactions, and engagement = authors + replies + reactions.
  5. Neighbours. Each piece keeps its session id, its position and the session's piece count, so a reader can open the pieces around it.

Why a 15-minute silence

A silence of more than 15 minutes starts a new session; 15 minutes or less keeps the talk together (gap > 15 * 60). 15 is a common default for chat, not tuned, and was measured afterwards on the 25,555 messages, channel by channel (D3, docs/DATA.md).

Shorter cuts a quarter of replies from what they answer (5 min) and leaves many one-message sessions. Longer puts three quarters or more of all messages into sessions over 40, which are then cut at a pause anyway, so it mostly glues together subjects a long silence separated. The curve has no sharp elbow: 15 minutes sits just past the 90th percentile of gaps and keeps 86% of replies with their parent, and the rest carry their parent as context (D4). It is a reasonable middle, not an optimum, and it was not tested against answer quality.

What it produced: 1,933 sessions became 2,362 conversations: median 4 messages, 90th percentile 33, at most 40. 124 sessions were cut, into 553 pieces; 51 of those are under 20 messages, because the last piece keeps the remainder. 1,277 conversations carry at least one context parent.

Why cut at all. 159 sessions have more than 30 messages and hold 67% of all messages. The largest is 1,054 messages from 98 authors over 12 hours, on update night, and the subject drifts about every 30 minutes inside a long session. One vector and one set of labels for that would be a blur.

The cost of cutting at pauses. The longest pause in the window can still be short. In the session above, the first cut lands on a 1.4-minute pause, because the next real silence (10.6 minutes) comes 44 messages in, past the 40 cap. A piece can still hold two subjects; cutting where the subject changes is the next step.

Decision · enrichment at ingest (D6, D7, D8, D16, D17, D23)

Topics are discovered from the data, revised by hand, and each is its own yes/no, asked by Jev

What a conversation is about, how it feels and what kind of conversation it is are closed questions. Jev (typesafe/jev-1.13, through OpenRouter) answers all of them about one conversation in a single request and returns calibrated probabilities over the options the code lists: nothing to parse, and no answer outside the list. The probabilities are stored; a topic or flag counts at 0.5, a rule written once in SQL (pulse_topics), so thresholds stay a query-time choice.

Jev answers the closed judgments (D9), and its main advantage here is that relabelling becomes so cheap that a user can start it whenever they want, so the team can make the topics its own without an engineer. Because a label is one yes/no per topic, changing one topic re-asks only that question: a full labelling run of all 2,362 conversations cost $0.23 to $0.24, and re-asking one topic is priced at about $0.06 (D6, D31).

Discovering topics. A channel-stratified sample of 300 conversations went to one Gemini Flash-Lite call, which suggested a label set and a sentiment target (data/work/suggested.json). It was revised by hand: Bushido split into its final update, Domains, and Ebontide and new quests; bugs and performance and pricing and editions added; "general franchise" became series direction; a generic "community chat" topic dropped. The list was fixed before any paid labelling. Descriptions were tightened with "only when ..." and "not ..." clauses after the first labels, and pricing again after the hand audit.

One yes/no per topic, not one choice. Each topic is its own question, so a conversation can carry several. 588 of the 2,362 conversations (25%) sit in two or more topics; 36.4% clear none and are "other". Counts by topic overlap, and the agent is told never to add topic rows into a total.

Three axes, kept apart. Topics say what a conversation is about and belong to the team. Flags say what kind it is, whatever the subject: excited and frustrated answer the brief directly (D8), beside bug, feature, help and noise. Sentiment says how people feel, towards a named target. So "frustrated conversations about Domains" is one filter of each.

Why topics and flags stay apart. One flat list of labels was the alternative. A question like "frustrations about Domains" crosses a subject with a kind, and a flat list has no subject axis to group by. So the two axes stay, and no topic may sit close to a flag, because two labels for one question disagree at the edges. The change was tested on 60 conversations before everything was relabelled. So "bugs and performance" was removed (bugs are the bug flag only), and "Other games and off-topic" became "Other games", leaving off-topic chatter to the noise flag (D17).

Sentiment towards what. The suggestion step proposed "Veil of Ages Bushido Final Update and Tides Remastered changes", which leaves out the rest of the series and the studio. A wider wording was also considered, "the Veil of Ages games and the studio behind them: their updates, releases and decisions". The target is "the Veil of Ages games and their developer" (D7): this is a game's Discord, so sentiment is towards the game or its developer, and the extra words added nothing. A cheerful meme thread about another game says nothing about how players feel about theirs.

The flag wording, v1 against v4 (D16)

v1 asked "does anyone ...". In a long chat someone is always happy once and grumpy once, so the flags stopped separating anything. v4 asks whether the feeling or request is a main thread of the conversation (in a one- or two-message conversation, the message itself). help counts a practical question that someone answers, even in a longer chat; opinion questions and banter do not. bug covers defects, crashes and performance.

Decision · retrieval (D10, D21)

Two retrieval tools, each with its own flow, and Jev judging relevance in both

The agent finds what to read through two tools. scan reads a whole slice of conversations: Jev judges every one against the question, so the answer rests on the full slice and its count covers all of it. find looks up a named thing: keywords and vectors in one SQL statement, fused by rank, then a Jev rerank. After the answer is written, Jev checks each cited claim against the message it cites.

The agent's instructions (src/lib/agent/instructions.ts) route by the shape of the question. How this rule came about, and what the agent called for each eval question, is in the Agent section.

Why hybrid search, and why Jev on top

Keywords catch exact names. "Ebontide", "fall damage" and "Switch 2" are words a conversation contains or does not. Postgres full-text search finds them exactly, and on the retrieval eval it was the stronger single retriever (nDCG 0.46 against 0.29 for vectors).

Vectors catch paraphrase. Players rarely use the question's words. The vector side matches on meaning: on "How hard is the new Domains mode?", the only arm that put a hand-picked gold message in its top 8 was vectors with the Jev rerank.

Fusion by rank. Reciprocal rank fusion merges the two lists by rank, so neither score scale has to be calibrated to the other, and a conversation both lists found rises.

Jev on top. Fusion orders candidates by how well they match. It does not know whether they answer. Jev reads each candidate with the question and answers one closed question, "does this help answer it?", with a calibrated probability. It writes no text, so there is nothing to parse, and the code thresholds the probability. On the eval the rerank added the most of any step: precision 54% to 74% and nDCG 0.59 to 0.78, for about 0.4 s at the median.

The same yes/no, three times. Inside scan, Jev is the relevance judge for every conversation in the slice. Inside find, it is the reranker. After the answer, it is the verifier: one request per cited claim, asking whether the message supports it (Claim check).

scan Read every conversation in the slice

For "what are people saying about X". The flow in src/lib/data/scan.ts, called by the scan tool in src/lib/agent/tools.ts.

SQL or codeJevGemini
  1. 1

    Filters code

    The model passes a question and a slice. Each filter becomes a numbered SQL parameter, never spliced text:

    • topic by key or by name: every conversation touching it at p ≥ 0.5 ('domains' = ANY(topics)). An unknown topic is answered with the real list.
    • flag: excited, frustrated, bug, feature or help, at p ≥ 0.5. A flag the question did not name is refused before anything is read.
    • channel, and author: conversations the person wrote at least one message in.
    • since inclusive and until exclusive, on the conversation's first message. A date that is not ISO is refused with words the model can act on.
  2. 2

    The slice, from Postgres SQL

    A count first, then every conversation in the slice with its transcript and labels. An empty slice returns "No conversations match these filters."

    Over 2,500 conversations? Refused, with the slice's conversations by topic and by week, so the agent narrows and calls again. This dataset has 2,362, so on it the branch never fires; it is there for a bigger export.
  3. 3

    Jev reads every conversation Jev

    One request per conversation, 40 in flight, with the question and the transcript (its first 12,000 characters). A call that fails is counted and reported with the result.

    The question, verbatim"Does conversation contain something that helps answer question: a direct answer, an opinion, a report or an experience that bears on it? Sharing a word or a topic with the question is not enough."
  4. 4

    Count, breakdowns, mood code

    A conversation is relevant at p ≥ 0.5. Every conversation in the slice was judged, so the relevant number is a count of the whole slice. The relevant ones are broken down by topic and by week. The slice's mood is the average stored sentiment of every conversation in it, from SQL.

  5. 5

    The top conversations, with their messages SQL

    The most relevant at p ≥ 0.3, ties to the more engaged: 10 by default, at most 15. Each comes with its messages and their [msgN] handles (text cut at 700 characters), plus any parent carried in as context.

  6. 6

    To the model Gemini

    A compact text: how many were read and how many are relevant ("Jev probability >= 50%"), the breakdowns, the mood, per-day rates over the period, then the top transcripts. The model writes the answer from it and cites the handles.

    The query Gemini

    The model writes what to look for in the community's own words, with optional slice filters (the same as scan's) and how many to return. A query with no words in it is replaced by the reader's own question.

  2. 2

    One SQL statement, two lists SQL

    Both lists run in the same query, under the same slice filters.

    Keywords, top 40The query's words through plainto_tsquery('english'), OR-ed: with AND, a question in plain English would match almost nothing. Matched on each conversation's full-text index (channel weighted A, transcript B, GIN) and ranked by ts_rank_cd.
    Vectors, top 40 Gemini embeddingThe query embedded by gemini-embedding-2 as RETRIEVAL_QUERY, 768 dimensions, L2-normalised. Ranked by cosine distance (<=>) on the HNSW index.
  3. 3

    Reciprocal rank fusion SQL

    Each conversation scores the sum of 1 / (60 + rank) over the lists it appears in. k = 60 is the standard constant; it dampens the gap between rank 1 and rank 2. The top 24 go on.

  4. 4

    Jev reranks each candidate Jev

    One request per candidate, all 24 in parallel, with the question and the transcript (its first 12,000 characters).

    The rerank question, verbatim"Does conversation contain information that helps answer question - a direct answer, an opinion, a report or an experience that bears on it? A conversation that only shares a word with the question does not help."
  5. 5

    Drop, order, cut code

    Anything under 0.25 is dropped, even if fewer than 8 remain. The rest are ordered by Jev's probability, then by the fused score: 8 by default, at most 12, each with its messages and [msgN] handles.

    If the rerank fails, the fused order stands, nothing is dropped, and the result says the rerank was unavailable.
  6. 6

    To the model Gemini

    "N relevant conversations (of 24 candidates)" and their transcripts, or "Nothing relevant found". No counts and no mood.

Inside find, step 2

How the vector side works

Every conversation is embedded once at ingest with gemini-embedding-2 at 768 dimensions, on the brief's key, as RETRIEVAL_DOCUMENT. A question is embedded at query time as RETRIEVAL_QUERY with the same model and dimensions. At 768 dimensions the returned vector is not unit length, so every vector is L2-normalised in code, on both sides, and cosine search compares like with like (D21).

The other tools

Seven typed tools in all (D10, D11). The agent reaches the data only through them and never writes SQL: a wrong generated query is a confident wrong number that changes from run to run. Counts come from SQL behind a fixed list of metrics.

Every tool with a slice takes the same filters as scan. An unknown topic is answered with the real list, a flag the question did not name is refused, and a read that fails on a dropped connection is tried once more.

The agent loop (D9)

One agent, a step cap that always ends in an answer, and a routing rule it learned

A ToolLoopAgent (AI SDK) on Gemini 2.5 Flash, at temperature 0.2, with the seven tools. The chat route and the eval build it through the same function, so the eval measures what a reader gets.

What it is given, and what bounds it

  • Instructions built from the data. The community, its dates, "now" and the relative periods counted back from it to the minute, which channel is about which game, and every topic by name and key, all read from the data. Some rules and examples still name this community's topics (the Domains, Ebontide, the "other" topics), and a relabel which renames or removes those topics would break them. The topic list itself is read from the data on every request; driving those rules from a "general" flag on the topic is on the roadmap (D20).
  • At most 8 steps. The last step gets no tools and the history flattened into one plain brief (the question and what each tool returned), so a model that keeps searching for something that is not there still ends with an answer.
  • A read before a "what do people say" answer. A turn that has only counted is made to read next; counts carry no messages to cite.
  • An empty turn is answered once from what the tools returned, then cited and checked like any answer.
  • Off-topic ends in a template. out_of_scope stops the turn and the app writes the reply. Before it is accepted, Jev asks whether the conversations bear on the question; at 0.5 or more the model answers from the data instead.
  • Questions are capped at 2,000 characters, in the page and in the route.

Its low price also makes relabelling cheap enough for a user to start (D6). Everything written (the answer, suggestions) stays on the provided key. It runs through OpenRouter's shared pool, which rate-limits under load (D28).

The routing rule: a broad question reads its topic, a named thing goes to find

Changed after a review of the answers. An answer about one topic had read every conversation in the database, because the agent scanned the whole window without the topic. The first fix sent any question about a topic to that topic's scan, and it also sent named things there, so the lookups got worse: in the earlier 18-question run, "How hard is the new Domains mode?" read every Domains conversation and led with the topic's mood (lookups 0.58). The instructions now route by the question's shape (D10; the two flows are drawn under Retrieval):

  • A broad question about a topic scans that topic's slice only: for multiplayer and co-op, the 4.1% of conversations carrying that topic instead of all 2,362.
  • A specific thing inside a topic (a named quest, item, boss or feature, one aspect of it, which bugs people hit, whether something is on a platform) goes to find first, with no topic filter.
  • Only a question with no subject ("what are people excited about") reads the whole window.
  • A topic read of under 150 conversations is followed by a find for recall, because a label misses a clear topic in about 1 of 5 conversations.

On a targeted re-run of the lookups the score rose from 0.58 to between 0.83 and 0.92 (commit 6ea3a56); in the 35-question run all 6 lookups were right.

What the agent called for each question of the earlier 18-question run (eval/results/agent.json, before the routing fix), with the judge's verdict. L01 and L02 are the routing failure; E01 counted before reading and scanned one topic at a time, which a later rule forbids.

Decision · quality after the answer (D11, D12)

Every cited claim is checked, in code and by Jev, before the reader relies on it

A citation that looks right but says something else is the worst failure a grounded product has, and nothing shows it unless something checks. Below is the real answer to eval question F01 from the earlier 18-question run (eval/results/agent.json), with its real citations; the headline numbers and the model comparison come from the 35-question run. Press the button to run the checks it went through.

7 of 7 cited claims backed by the messages they cite
1

Normalise every handle code

Citations are short handles the app owns, [msg21527]. Whatever the model writes ((msg12), [MSG12; msg40], a bare msg12) is normalised, and anything else that looks like an id is dropped.

2

Does the id exist, and did a tool show it in this chat? code

A made-up id is removed, and so is one no tool returned in this chat. A follow-up may cite what an earlier turn read. In this run: 19 citations, 0 made up, 0 unseen.

3

Does the message support the sentence? Jev

One Jev request per cited claim, backed at p ≥ 0.5. A sentence with two sides puts each citation to its own side. A sentence that lists things is also asked whether one message backs all of it. A failed call marks the claim "not checked", said apart, never rewritten.

The question, verbatim"Does the message evidence.X SUPPORT claim - does it say, show or clearly imply what the claim attributes to it? … A message that says the opposite, disagrees with it, or is only about the same subject without saying it, does NOT support it."
4

Figures must come from a tool that ran code

A figure in a cited claim that neither its messages nor any tool contains fails the claim. A number tagged scan, aggregate or voices fails unless that tool gave a result in the chat, and is named under the answer. Per-day rates and "rose" or "fell" are compared with what the tools computed.

5

Weak claims are rewritten once Flash-Lite

The flagged claims and what they cite go to one rewrite: fix or drop each, change nothing else. It is checked again and kept only if it changed words, still cites, is at least as well supported and states no more unmatched figures. Nothing to rewrite on this answer.

6

How many conversations back each claim Jev

In the app, up to 8 claims are put to the 80 most relevant conversations the turn found, 12 messages each: "5 conversations say this: 2 cited, 3 more". Reads are logged, retried on a 429, 5xx or timeout, and run 8 at a time (D28). It changes no text, so the eval skips it.

Before the checks. An answer that read messages but cites none goes back once to a cite pass. Whatever is still uncited says so under it ("Counts only"). One function (afterAgent) runs all of this for the chat route and the eval alike.

On the reader's page. A line under the answer says how many claims are backed; fewer than half backed shows a warning, never a check mark (D27). "Some wording was tightened" appears only when a kept rewrite changed words.

Known gaps in v1.0 (docs/QA.md). The check asks whether a message supports its sentence, so it passes a supported sentence about another game: a frustrations answer built its pricing section from #off-topic talk about GTA 6 prices, and all 18 claims passed (P2). Some paraphrases whose meaning drifted also pass (P9). A follow-up that calls no tool and cites nothing is not checked at all (P3).

The product surface (D14, D18, D31, D32)

The chat page is where the answer is read; Explore is the grid behind it

The brief asks for a useful answer grounded in the messages and calls anything more a bonus, so the minutes went to the answer path (retrieval, grounding, the claim check), and the UI shows what that path did. The page speaks Discord's words: channels, messages, reactions. Production runs the chat and a read-only Explore grid; login and topic editing are not on main.

Ask

  • The answer streams in with citation chips; a chip opens the message it cites.
  • One line says what the agent did ("read 109 frustrated conversations from 24 Sep"), with its steps a click away. A scan or aggregate tag in the answer links to its step, and a step can carry one small chart.
  • The verification is shown: how many claims are backed (a warning when fewer than half are), which were rewritten, and how many conversations back each claim.
  • The evidence panel opens a cited conversation as a reply tree: reading order across, reply depth down. A session's top-level messages hang from one node for the conversation, replies nest beneath the message they answer, the cited messages are numbered like their chips and lit, and a parent carried in from an earlier piece is drawn hollow and faint (D32). On a phone it opens as a bottom sheet. There are no Discord permalinks: the export has none.
  • Saved chats survive a reload at /c/[id], with past chats in a sidebar, owned by an anonymous cookie id: no accounts, each browser sees its own history. The first cut had no history; a review of the page as its user found no list of previous conversations, and a community manager returns to an answer to quote it (D18).
  • The brief's three questions are the starters, and each topic is one chip away.

Explore: topic by week

Conversations touching each topic, by the week they start, read from the database. A conversation in two topics sits in both rows, so the rows add up to more than the total. The first week holds only 13 Sep 19:44 onward.

In the app the grid shows how many conversations or their mood, by day, week or month. Selecting cells lists the busiest sessions and the voices in them, and prefills a question for the chat. Known in v1.0: a "Busiest conversations" row asks the chat about an internal handle and gets an empty answer (P1); Explore's prefilled question lower-cases topic names (P7); the busiest rows are sessions, so they can exceed 40 messages (P15).

Topic editing: built, and held back from production (D31)

On the branch explore-topic-editing, the team adds, renames, redefines, combines or removes topics in Explore. A rename or a combine applies at once. Adding or redefining a topic shows a price first; on confirm, Jev asks that one topic's yes/no question of every conversation and backfills the probabilities, in resumable batches, under a spend cap the team sets. Removing a topic is free.

What it costs. The branch prices a relabel before any call as conversations x (state + question + call overhead) tokens at Jev's $0.042 per million input tokens. With this data's mean state of 1,321 characters, re-asking one topic of all 2,362 conversations comes to 1.30 to 1.55 million tokens, about $0.06, a formula result no run has checked yet. The measured upper bound is a full labelling run, every topic, flag and sentiment: $0.23 to $0.24 for each of the three runs recorded.

Why it is not on main. The grid only reads the labels; editing writes them, and its tables and any relabel would run against the one Neon database production shares. No Neon branch could be made to test on before delivery, and code that has never run does not go to main (D1).

DecisionsThe evidence panel's first picture, a timeline of dots with arcs for replies, was not good enough; the reply tree replaced it (D32). Chats are saved, with a list of past ones (D18). The Explore grid went to production. The relabel and backfill had never run against a database, and production shares the only one, so all writes to Neon stopped; adding untested writes just before delivery was not worth the risk, and editing is first on the roadmap with the reason Jev was chosen (D31, D6).

Known limits

  • Conversation pieces are cut at pauses, not at subject changes; a piece can still hold two subjects.
  • Labels were audited on a small sample, read by hand by the builder, and the pricing fix was not re-audited at scale. Topics miss a clear subject in about 1 of 5 conversations, so topic counts run low; the agent follows a topic read with a search.
  • Reactions are too sparse to measure reach; engagement is a proxy.
  • The eval is small and written by the builder.
  • Explore is read-only on main: topic editing is built but held back on a branch (roadmap, item 1).
  • No login: each browser sees its own saved chats. No Discord permalinks: the export has none.
  • Jev runs on a key outside the one provided, through OpenRouter's shared pool, which rate-limits under load (D28).

Found by the production QA of v1.0

Left open because production is frozen (docs/QA.md, where all 18 P findings are logged).

  • Explore's "Busiest conversations" rows return an empty answer (P1). A row asks the chat about an internal handle ("What did people say in conv1268 ...?"); the model returns nothing, and the page says the answer was not finished. The handle also shows in the chat's title.
  • The claim check can pass an off-topic citation (P2). A frustrations answer built its pricing section from #off-topic talk about GTA 6 prices, and all its claims passed. The check asks only whether the message supports the sentence, and here it did.
  • A follow-up can answer without reading (P3). "Which of those are bugs?" answered "none are bugs" with no tool call, no citations and no check, although the data has a bug flag and other answers found bugs in the same week.
  • Post suggestions are thin (P4). "What should we post about this week?" gave three ideas, each on one message, with no engagement measure behind them.
  • A long chat stops answering from the 7th question on, intermittently (P18). The route sends the model the last 12 messages; from the 7th question that window can open on an earlier answer that began with a tool call, and Gemini rejects a tool call that no question precedes. The input stays enabled and "Try again" repeats the error. The fix: start the window at a question (route.ts:86).
  • Dates written in messages disagree with the timestamps (P8). The export looks time-shifted, so an answer can quote "a 9 July launch" as upcoming on 27 September.

Roadmap, in priority order

  1. Finish and merge topic editing. Built on explore-topic-editing, held back because it writes labels and the only database is production's (D31). This is a main reason Jev was chosen (D6): re-asking one topic of all 2,362 conversations is priced at about $0.06 (a formula, not yet run; a full labelling run cost $0.23 to $0.24), cheap enough for the team to start a relabel whenever it wants, without an engineer. To finish: make a Neon branch and point .env.local at it; npm run db:app; npm run labels -- seed-spend, so ingest's cost is counted in the same ledger; in the app, price, run and remove a topic, and check that the grid and the agent read the new labels; merge, then deploy.
  2. Fix what the production QA found: open an Explore row's conversation in the evidence panel, and let an empty model turn fall through to the forced answer (P1); ask in the claim check, or in the scan, whether a cited message is about this community's games (P2); make a follow-up that asks for a slice call a tool (P3); rank post ideas by engagement and ground each in more than one conversation, or say the evidence is thin (P4); quote dates from messages as written (P8); start the history window at a question, and load the history on the server instead of resending every tool output (P18).
  3. Drive topic rules from data, not names. The prompt and the chart code name the general topics ("other", other games) to leave them out of excitement and resonance rankings (D20), and the prompt's examples name the Domains and Ebontide. After a relabel those names can be gone. A general flag on a topic should drive the exclusion, and the examples should come from the active topic set.
  4. Re-audit the labels after the pricing fix, by a person, on a larger sample.
  5. A sturdier eval: questions written by someone who has not read the data, a second judge, several runs per model.
  6. Semantic segmentation: cut long sessions where the subject changes, not where the pause is longest.
  7. Many communities: the pipeline reads a manifest per dataset and an adapter per platform (one exists, for this Discord export); partition the tables by community and move labelling to a queue.
  8. Streaming ingest: label and embed new messages as they arrive instead of a batch rebuild.
